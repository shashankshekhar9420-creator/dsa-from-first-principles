5. Queue vs Deque

It is tempting to think:

"So is a deque just a better queue?"

Not exactly.

A deque is more flexible.

A queue imposes a particular rule:

add
→
back
remove
→
front

A deque allows:

add/remove ← either end →

So you can use a deque as a queue.

For example:

Python code (click to hide or show)
from collections import deque

q = deque()

q.append("A")
q.append("B")
q.append("C")

q.popleft()

This behaves like a normal FIFO queue.

But you can also use the same structure when you need both ends.

That's an important general DSA idea:

A more flexible data structure can implement a simpler one.

A deque can behave like a queue.

A deque can also behave like a stack.

But its real value appears when we genuinely need access to both ends.

7. But Why Not Just Use a Queue?

Good question.

If the window moves like a queue, why did we need to learn a deque?

Because we're about to encounter a second requirement.

Suppose the window is:

2
1
5

and we want:

the maximum

The answer is:

5

A queue can tell us that:

2 is the oldest
1 came after it
5 came after that

But it cannot efficiently tell us:

5 is the largest

So we need to maintain some information about the values.

And this is where the two ends of a deque are going to become much more interesting.

8. An Important Thought Experiment

Consider:

2
1
5

We want the maximum.

Now ask:

Do we actually need to keep every element equally?

Suppose 5 has just entered.

Compare:

2 vs 5
1 vs 5

We know:

2 < 5
1 < 5

So while 5 remains inside the window:

  • 2 cannot become the maximum.
  • 1 cannot become the maximum.

They are already beaten by a newer element.

This gives us our first major clue:

Some elements inside a window can become permanently irrelevant.

That's a very different idea from an ordinary queue.

A queue says:

"Everyone waits their turn."

But for a maximum problem, we don't necessarily care about everyone.

We care about the elements that could still become the maximum.

9. The Idea of "Candidates"

Let's look at:

2
1
5

If we're looking for the maximum, the useful candidate is:

5

because it's currently the largest.

The 2 and 1 are weaker candidates.

But don't jump too quickly to:

"Delete everything smaller."

We need to be careful.

Consider:

5
3
4

The current maximum is:

5

But 3 might still matter later.

Why?

Because 5 could eventually leave the window.

If that happens:

5
↑ 5 expires
3
 
4
 

then the candidates for the maximum become:

3
4

and 4 wins.

So we can't simply keep only the current maximum.

We need to keep the useful candidates.

That is the real problem.

10. What Makes an Element Useless?

Let's compare two elements:

3
4

Suppose 4 arrives after 3.

We have:

3
4

Ask:

While 4 is inside the window, can 3 ever become the maximum?

No.

Why?

Because:

3 < 4

And 4 is newer.

Therefore, 3 has two disadvantages:

  1. It's smaller.
  2. It will leave no later than 4.

So 3 can never beat 4.

This is a powerful observation.

A newer element that is larger than an older element completely dominates the older element.

And once an element is dominated like this, we don't need to keep it as a candidate.

11. Why the Back of the Deque Matters

Now our need for both ends begins to make sense.

Suppose our useful candidates are:

Notice what happened.

We:

  • looked at the front for the best candidate,
  • removed useless candidates from the back,
  • added the new candidate at the back.

That requires access to both ends.

And suddenly the deque isn't just a fancy queue anymore.

It is becoming exactly the structure we need.

12. Don't Name the Pattern Yet

At this point, we could say:

"This is a monotonic deque."

But that's not useful yet.

The name doesn't explain anything.

Instead, notice what we have discovered naturally.

We want our candidates to look like:

largest
smaller
smaller
...

For example:

9
7
5
2

The values decrease as we move from front to back.

Why?

Because then:

The best candidate is always at the front.

And when a new element arrives, we can remove smaller candidates from the back.

That is the idea we'll formalize in the next article.

13. The Bigger Lesson

There is actually a useful DSA principle hiding here.

We started with:

"I need the maximum of every sliding window."

The naive reaction might be:

"I'll keep the whole window and find the maximum."

Then we asked:

"Do I really need every element?"

And discovered:

No.

Some elements can be permanently ruled out.

That leads to a broader principle:

Don't maintain information merely because it exists. Maintain information that can still affect the answer.

This is one of the most important ideas behind the monotonic deque.

The deque is not valuable because it's a deque.

It's valuable because it gives us a convenient place to maintain our remaining candidates.

14. Where We Are Now

We have built the idea step by step.

That is exactly the shape of a monotonic deque.
